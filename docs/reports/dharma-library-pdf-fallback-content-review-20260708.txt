## Step 1 — Confirm the file exists

```bash
find /opt/agents/library/buddhism -iname "*<keyword>*" 2>/dev/null
```

This will show both the indexed `.pdf` and `.json` in the library root, plus the Calibre copy under `/opt/agents/library/buddhism/calibre/`.

## Step 2 — Convert PDF to text

```bash
pdftotext "/opt/agents/library/buddhism/<subcategory>/<Filename>.pdf" /tmp/output.txt && echo "done"
```

Prefer the indexed copy (not Calibre) — it's already in the subcategory folder and was the one indexed.

## Step 3 — Read strategically

Use `read_file` with offset and limit to sample the text. The file will have thousands of lines.

- Lines 1–100: front matter, table of contents
- Lines 200–400: preface, intro
- Lines 1000+: core doctrinal content begins
- Scan in 80-line windows, moving forward until you find relevant material

```python
read_file(path="/tmp/output.txt", offset=1800, limit=80)
```

## Step 4 — Cite correctly

Once you find the passage, synthesize in your own voice and cite naturally:
"según Gueshe Tashi Tsering en El Gran Vacío..."

Do NOT quote verbatim unless the passage is short and the user explicitly asks for the exact words.

## Multi-file triangulation

When `library-search` surfaces 3–5 candidate files for a complex doctrinal question:

1. Convert all candidates with `pdftotext` (fast, ~1–2s each)
2. Run `grep -n` with relevant keywords across all converted files before reading any
3. Identify which file has the densest, most specific hits — read that one first
4. Use `read_file` only on the 2–3 most promising line ranges per file
5. Stop when you have enough for a well-grounded synthesis — you don't need to exhaust every file

This is faster than converting one file, reading it fully, then moving to the next.

## Pitfalls

- Full Qdrant scroll (1.1M+ points) times out at 180s — cap at 300k for source enumeration; unique sources stabilize well before that (649 found in first 300k)
- `pdftotext` on scanned PDFs may return empty or garbled text — check the first 20 lines after conversion
- Some PDFs use form feeds (`\f`) as page separators — these appear as blank lines in read_file output, normal behavior
- `python3 -c "import fitz..."` commands may time out in this environment — prefer `pdftotext` via shell
- library-search has a cold-start cost (loads 768-dim embedding model) — one call max per query, never loop it
- `Meditation_on_Emptiness.pdf` (Jeffrey Hopkins) produces hundreds of "Invalid Font Weight" stderr warnings during pdftotext — pipe stderr to /dev/null or the actual grep results will be buried: `pdftotext file.pdf output.txt 2>/dev/null`
- `library-search --source <pdf> --chunk <N>` flags do NOT work — they return empty output. Use pdftotext + grep instead to retrieve specific chunks from a known file.
- For complex doctrinal questions with multiple keyword variants, cast a wide grep net in one call before windowing: `grep -n -i 'term1\|term2\|term3' /tmp/file.txt | head -60` — this is faster than running multiple greps sequentially.
- When searching multiple PDFs for the same topic, convert all of them first (in parallel via execute_code), then grep all — avoids repeated tool round-trips.
- Glossary/word-list sections at the end of PDFs can produce false positives for Tibetan term searches (e.g. `kun gzhi'i rnam shes` appearing only in a vocabulary list). Check surrounding context before reading a window around a hit.

# Free Will and Agency — Cross-Tradition Synthesis
*Built July 2026 from: Geshe Tashi Tsering Buddhist Psychology vol.3 (mahayana/), Four Noble Truths vol.1 (mahayana/), Nāgārjuna Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (madhyamaka/), Tsongkhapa Final Exposition of Wisdom (madhyamaka/), Longchenpa Precious Treasury of Philosophical Systems (dzogchen/). Key PDF line numbers given for re-extraction.*

---

## 1. Abhidharma / Gelug Psychology — cetanā as the hinge

**Core claim:** The relevant Buddhist category is not "free will" but *cetanā* (volition/intention) — the always-present mental factor that coordinates and directs the mind toward its object. Cetanā IS the karmic action; body and speech create karma only insofar as the mental act is present.

**Where intention comes from:** Karmic imprints (vāsanā) accumulated from beginningless time act as habit-patterns that color feeling and dictate the direction of intention. The decision is not random but is also not free in any absolute sense — it is the conditioned fruit of prior conditioning.

**Buddhist Psychology vol.3, lines 1471–1540 (key passage):**
> "Intention, the next always-present mental factor, is also called volition. This is the element that coordinates and directs the activity of each of the other elements within the main mind in respect to the object... intention, or volition, is a karmic action... New karma is created in every moment of consciousness because intention is always present."
> "Where does intention come from?... From beginningless time, through our actions we have collected karmic imprints on our mindstreams, and these dictate our intentions."

**Four Noble Truths vol.1, lines 870–930 (karma/volition):**
> "Only when there has been some sort of mental action, some sort of intention or volition, does the specific chain reaction of cause and result occur... due to ignorance there is volition."
> Lines 2800–2830: "Volitional action is a culmination of ignorance and craving, and clearly implies the involvement of some kind of will. From ignorance and craving comes karma, or volitional action."

**The "freedom" available:** A trained mind has greater capacity to observe the gap between contact and habitual intention and redirect it. This is freedom *within* causality, not *from* it.

---

## 2. Theravāda — responsibility without a fixed self

Karma presupposes moral responsibility, but the responsible entity is a continuum, not a fixed agent. The continuum is transformable through practice. The "freedom" is the margin of transformability — a more lucid mind conditions the next moment more skillfully.

---

## 3. Madhyamaka Prāsaṅgika — mutual dependence of agent and action

**Nāgārjuna, MMK Chapter VIII (lines 1310–1415 in nagarjuna.txt):**

Key argument (verses 1–13):
- An inherently existing agent cannot perform action (already fixed; no change possible)
- A non-existing agent cannot perform action (no cause)
- Agent and action are mutually dependent: "Action depends upon the agent. The agent itself depends on action. One cannot see any way to establish them differently." (v.12)
- Neither has svabhāva (inherent existence)

**What remains:** A *conventionally designated* person acting within dependent origination — valid at the conventional level, empty of inherent existence at the ultimate level.

**Tsongkhapa, Final Exposition of Wisdom, lines 3960–3985:**
> "even though [phenomena] do not inherently exist, it is permissible to posit agents, activities, and objects — bondage and liberation, cause and effect, object comprehended and comprehender, and so forth — with respect to them."

Lines 2955–2985 (actions not wasted): If the self were inherently established, actions done by one continuum-moment could not be experienced by a later moment — Nāgārjuna's refutation of an inherent self preserves karmic continuity.

**Summary position:** Free will is a *conventionally valid* designation. Neither determinism (no fixed agent) nor libertarianism (no uncaused cause). The question itself presupposes a kind of selfhood that doesn't withstand analysis.

---

## 4. Yogācāra / Chittamātra — agent as reflexive consciousness

**Longchenpa, Precious Treasury of Philosophical Systems, lines 3395–3415:**
> "Chittamatrins hold that the agent of experience is simply a state of reflexive consciousness that is naturally lucid, without the duality of objects and the subjective agent perceiving those objects."

The ālayavijñāna (basis-consciousness) stores karmic seeds; the sense of "I who choose" (manas/kliṣṭamanas) is the afflictive distortion of this reflexive awareness. What seems like a free center of choice is the misapprehension of a naturally luminous but duality-structured process.

**Vatsiputrīya view (refuted by Longchenpa, lines 2830–2870):** The self is neither eternal nor impermanent, is the agent that carries karmic continuity. Longchenpa shows this is internally inconsistent: if not eternal and not impermanent, what is it?

---

## 5. Dzogchen — beyond the frame of cause and result

**Longchenpa, Precious Treasury of Philosophical Systems, lines 11325–11345:**
> "Natural great perfection, the essence of utterly lucid basic space, is naturally occurring timeless awareness. Since it involves neither cause nor result, neither something to develop nor an agent to develop it..."

Lines 11870–11895 (White Expanse):
> "Because conditioned sensory appearances are naturally free, they are effortlessly devoid of any cause; in the way that they abide, they cannot be established to be anything at all."

Lines 13330–13365:
> "The skillful means by which one rests imperturbably and effortlessly in awareness is trekcho, timeless freedom... Through familiarization with these, one abides in the certainty of this freedom, which is timelessly the case but simply becomes evident at this point."

**The radical claim:** The question of free will belongs to the register of a mind that has not recognized Rigpa. Within recognition, there is no agent that needs to be free, and no causality that traps it. The "effortlessness" of Dzogchen is not freedom from causality but the dissolution of the knower-who-could-be-determined. Appearances are "naturally free" not because they lack causes, but because in recognition their nature does not generate the grip that makes freedom/determination a live question.

**Crucial nuance (Longchenpa, lines 255–265):** This view is soteriologically dangerous without prior training. Treating effortlessness as license ("emptiness without karma") leads to downfall. The realization that renders causality irrelevant is itself causally produced by the path.

---

## Cross-tradition tension (not resolved)

All schools affirm: the path produces results; practice matters; the practitioner's choices have consequences. But:

| Tradition | Who practices? | With what freedom? |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| Abhidharma | Conditioned continuum with trainable margin | Freedom within causality |
| Prāsaṅgika | Conventionally designated person | Valid convention, empty of svabhāva |
| Yogācāra | Ālaya-stream transforming its own seeds | No independent agent; transformation is structural |
| Dzogchen | Recognition dissolves the question | Timeless freedom; the path is for arriving at the non-problem |

**The irreducible paradox:** Free will as philosophical question lives in the register where there is still a "self" that could be free or unfree. Buddhist traditions don't answer whether that self is free — they methodically dissolve the coherence of the self that poses the question.

# Free Will — Multi-Tradition Buddhist Knowledge Bank

Synthesized July 2026 from: Buddhist Psychology vol. 3 (Geshe Tashi Tsering), The Four Noble Truths vol. 1 (Geshe Tashi Tsering), Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (Nāgārjuna, Jay Garfield translation), Precious Treasury of Philosophical Systems (Longchenpa), Tsong-kha-pa's Final Exposition of Wisdom.

---

## 1. Abhidharma / Pāli baseline: cetanā

**Key claim:** Karma is not action per se but *volition* (cetanā). Every moment of consciousness contains intention; intention is the karmic act. The body and speech create karma only as perceived by the mental act.

**Source passages (Buddhist Psychology vol. 3, pdftotext lines 1471–1540):**
- "Intention, the next always-present mental factor, is also called volition. This is the element that coordinates and directs the activity of each of the other elements within the main mind."
- "intention, or volition, is a karmic action... New karma is created in every moment of consciousness because intention is always present."
- "Where does intention come from? ...From beginningless time, through our actions we have collected karmic imprints on our mindstreams, and these dictate our intentions."

**Implication for free will:** Volition is real and causally efficacious, but it is *determined* by prior karmic imprints. No uncaused free choice. The space for practice is the transformability of the habit-stream — not freedom *from* causes but a different kind of cause (attention, meditation) that can reshape future intentions.

**Four Noble Truths vol. 1 (lines 920–926):** "due to ignorance there is volition" — volitional action is a culmination of ignorance and craving. Clearly implies the involvement of some kind of will, but will that is conditioned.

---

## 2. Madhyamaka: the agent has no inherent existence

**Key claim:** Neither the agent nor the action exists inherently. They are mutually dependent designations. The self-as-chooser is a conventional imputation with no ontological foundation.

**Nāgārjuna, Mūlamadhyamakakārikā Ch. VIII (lines 1310–1415 of pdftotext):**
- "This existent agent does not perform an existent action. Nor does some nonexistent agent perform some nonexistent action."
- "Action depends upon the agent. The agent itself depends on action. One cannot see any way to establish them differently."
- Conclusion: agent and action arise in mutual dependence — neither is primary, neither has intrinsic existence.

**Tsongkhapa, Final Exposition of Wisdom (lines 3960–3980):**
- "even though [phenomena] do not inherently exist, it is permissible to posit agents, activities, and objects — bondage and liberation, cause and effect, object comprehended and comprehender."
- The conventional agent is valid *as a designation*. Karma, moral responsibility, and the path all function at this level.
- If the self were inherently established, earlier and later moments could not form a single continuum → actions would be wasted or misassigned across persons. The *lack* of inherent existence is what makes karma coherent across lives.

**Philosophical position:** Neither libertarian free will (no uncaused agent) nor hard determinism (conventional agency functions). The Prāsaṅgika position is closest to *compatibilism* — agency is conventionally real, metaphysically empty.

---

## 3. Yogācāra: ālaya-vijñāna and the illusory center of choice

**Key claim:** The apparent subject who "chooses" is a refraction of reflexive consciousness. The ālaya-vijñāna (basis-consciousness) stores karmic seeds and generates the illusion of a unified choosing self.

**Longchenpa on Chittamātra (Precious Treasury of Philosophical Systems, lines 3395–3415):**
- "Chittamatrins hold that the agent of experience is simply a state of reflexive consciousness that is naturally lucid, without the duality of objects and the subjective agent perceiving those objects."
- The sense of "I choose" arises from the afflictive aspect of consciousness — investigation generating attraction, aversion, or indifference.

**Philosophical position:** The felt experience of free will is a constructed appearance of consciousness. Practice transforms the seeds in the ālaya; there is no transcendent chooser.

---

## 4. Dzogchen: beyond cause and result — the frame itself dissolves

**Key claim:** The question of free will presupposes a subject operating within cause and effect. From the Dzogchen perspective, this frame is itself the confusion.

**Longchenpa, Precious Treasury of Philosophical Systems (lines 11325–11345, 11870–11890):**
- "Natural great perfection, the essence of utterly lucid basic space, is naturally occurring timeless awareness. Since it involves neither cause nor result, neither something to develop nor an agent to develop it..."
- "Because conditioned sensory appearances are naturally free, they are effortlessly devoid of any cause."
- Awakened mind is "beyond cause and result" — this is presented as the specific superiority of Atiyoga over Anuyoga.
- Trekchö: "timeless freedom... the skillful means by which one rests imperturbably and effortlessly in awareness."

**Philosophical position:** The practitioner does not "choose" to liberate thoughts — liberation is the intrinsic nature of thoughts when their ground (Rigpa) is recognized. The question of free will belongs to the mind that has not yet recognized its own nature.

---

## 5. Structural tension (not resolved)

All schools affirm that practice produces results — the path matters. But the question of *who practices* and *with what freedom* gets different answers:
- Abhidharma: a trainable mind within conditioned streams
- Prāsaṅgika: a conventional designation that is sufficient for the path
- Yogācāra: transforming the seeds of the ālaya
- Dzogchen: recognition that there was never a practitioner who needed liberation — yet the path is still necessary to arrive at that recognition

The paradox is deliberate: free will as a philosophical question lives at the level where there is still a "self" that might or might not be free. The traditions don't answer whether that self is free; they dissolve the consistency of the self that asks the question.
